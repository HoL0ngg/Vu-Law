PAYMENT OBLIGATIONS: WHY ARE CLAIMS FOR PRINCIPAL, INTEREST, CONTRACTUAL PENALTIES AND DAMAGES DETERMINED DIFFERENTLY?
TOPIC 02 | IDENTIFYING THE LEGAL BASIS AND FORMULATING PAYMENT CLAIMS
Building on our introductory article, “A Favourable Judgment Does Not Guarantee Recovery”, Integritas Vu Legal presents a series of in-depth Legal Insights examining the key legal and practical considerations involved in pursuing and enforcing payment claims, from the legal characterisation of disputes, dispute resolution strategies, evidential assessment and asset preservation to judgment enforcement and the distinction between civil and criminal liability where relevant.
Before an outstanding receivable can be pursued through legal proceedings, it is necessary to identify correctly the legal nature of the transaction, the party liable, the principal amount that has fallen due, each category of interest and the conditions for imposing contractual remedies. An error in any of these respects may affect limitation periods, evidence, the amount claimed and the enforceability of the judgment.
01 | CORRECTLY IDENTIFYING THE LEGAL RELATIONSHIP IN DISPUTE
Descriptions such as “outstanding debt” or “unpaid balance” are not, in themselves, sufficient to determine the legal relationship. The same transfer of funds may constitute a loan, an advance payment for goods, a deposit, a progress payment or an amount repayable upon the invalidity of a transaction. The matters to be examined are the basis on which the obligation arose, the purpose of the transfer, the conditions governing when the obligation falls due and the manner in which the contract was performed.
An incorrect characterisation may result in: (i) the petition of an inapplicable sector-specific law or interest provision; (ii) an incorrect determination of the limitation period or competent dispute resolution body; (iii) a claim for an inappropriate contractual remedy; (iv) the omission of necessary participants in the proceedings or evidence establishing the conditions giving rise to the obligation; and (v) the selection of measures to preserve assets that are inconsistent with the rights in dispute.
However, it would be inaccurate to suggest that every error in describing the disputed legal relationship necessarily leads to dismissal of the claim. The court is responsible for determining the legal relationship to be resolved on the basis of the relief sought and the facts of the case. The particular consequences depend on the case and the stage of the proceedings.
Importantly, not every dispute arising from a contract is exclusively civil or commercial in nature. In certain circumstances, a transaction may involve deception or misappropriation of property and require assessment under criminal law. If a business pursues civil proceedings alone while disregarding such indications, it risks choosing an unsuitable means of protecting its interests. Section 13 of Guidance No. 25/HD-VKSTC of 2022 of the Supreme People’s Procuracy refers to cases exhibiting indications of criminal conduct that were nevertheless resolved through civil proceedings, resulting in judgments or decisions being quashed or amended. [1]
Nevertheless, a failure to fulfil a payment obligation does not automatically constitute a criminal offence. Equally, an inaccurate description of the disputed legal relationship by the claimant is not, of itself, a ground for quashing a judgment. The legal consequences depend on the nature of the transaction, the evidence and the actual errors occurring in the resolution of the case.
Accordingly, the value of legal advice lies not only in advocacy during contentious proceedings but, first and foremost, in correctly identifying the nature of the matter, the relevant parties, the scope of the claim and an appropriate dispute resolution mechanism from the outset. This provides a basis for mitigating procedural risks, preserving evidence and improving the effectiveness of receivables recovery.
02 | IDENTIFYING THE LIABLE PARTY AND THE EXTENT OF LIABILITY
A business may correctly identify an outstanding receivable yet fail to achieve recovery if it directs its payment claim against the wrong party. In practice, the person who signs the contract, the person who actually receives the funds, and the person legally liable for payment are not necessarily the same.
Under Article 87 of the 2015 Civil Code, a juridical person bears civil liability with its own assets for obligations established or performed by its representative in its name. A representative or capital-contributing member is not automatically required to use personal assets to discharge those obligations. Exceptions involving personal liability, transactions exceeding the scope of representation and security for obligations must be examined. [2]
The regime governing liability with assets also depends on the type of enterprise. For a sole proprietorship, clause 1 Article 188 of the 2020 Law on Enterprises provides that its owner is liable with all of their assets for all activities of the enterprise. This liability is not limited to the registered investment capital and does not cease merely because the owner hires another person to act as director (Article 190). Even where the sole proprietorship is sold, the former owner remains, in principle, liable for debts arising before the transfer, unless otherwise agreed, pursuant to clause 2 Article 192 of the 2020 Law on Enterprises. [3]
In disputes involving guarantees or mortgages over assets belonging to third parties, the borrower’s repayment obligation must be distinguished from the scope of the guarantor’s obligation and the limits on realisation of the secured assets. Where a third party mortgages its assets, enforcement is limited to the actual value of the assets validly provided and registered as security; absent a different agreement, the secured party has no right of recourse against the mortgagor’s other assets for any shortfall. By contrast, under a guarantee, the guarantor undertakes to perform all or part of the principal obligor’s obligation if the latter fails to perform, or performs improperly, when due; depending on the terms of the undertaking, that liability may extend to all of the guarantor’s assets. Failure to distinguish these arrangements may result in improperly formulated claims, inappropriate applications for interim emergency measures or other legal risks.
In particular, a guarantor and a person who provides assets as security for another person’s obligation do not automatically bear the same extent of liability. Under Articles 335, 336 and 339 of the 2015 Civil Code, a guarantee must be assessed by reference to the scope of the undertaking and the conditions and time for its performance. Likewise, the realisation of mortgaged assets must be determined by the security transaction and applicable law. [2]
A receivables recovery strategy must therefore address not only “Who must pay?” but also “To what extent, with which assets and subject to what conditions?” Reviewing the counterparty’s legal status, the basis of the obligation and the assets against which liability may be enforced from the outset is important for mitigating procedural risks and improving the effectiveness of judgment enforcement.
03 | PRINCIPAL: AN OUTSTANDING BALANCE MUST BE SUBSTANTIATED
In disputes over receivables, an amount entered in a business’s accounting records is not automatically an amount that a court may order the other party to pay. Each amount claimed must be established by reference to an obligation that has actually arisen, its due date and the corresponding supporting evidence.
For contracts for the sale of goods, provision of services or construction works, the principal amount must be reconciled against each order, invoice, delivery or acceptance milestone. The amount claimed must take account of payments already made, valid set-offs, returned goods, price adjustments and agreements varying the obligation. In particular, the contract price must not be equated with the amount actually owed, as the payment obligation may arise only in respect of goods delivered or work performed in accordance with the conditions agreed by the parties.
As regards evidence, debt reconciliation records and acceptance certificates are important documents, but they are not the only relevant evidence. Depending on the transaction, contracts, delivery documents, invoices, bank statements, emails, electronic data and the parties’ performance of the contract may be considered together to establish the payment obligation. Conversely, the issuance of an invoice or recognition of revenue does not, by itself, prove that the entire disputed amount has fallen due and remains unpaid.
Another frequently overlooked risk is including amounts not yet due in the total amount claimed. Where a contract provides for payment by instalments, default on one instalment does not automatically cause all remaining instalments to fall due. The contractual payment terms, any right to demand early performance and the relevant legal provisions must be examined.
Before commencing proceedings, the principal amount should therefore be supported by a reconciliation schedule recording, for each transaction, the amount, supporting documents, due date and payment status. This helps determine the precise scope of the claim, provides a basis for calculating late-payment interest and assessing the evidence, and reduces the risk of a claim being dismissed because the basis or amount of the obligation has not been established.
A receivable may be accurately recorded for accounting purposes without being adequately substantiated in law. This distinction explains why businesses should review the case file and formulate their payment claims before entering into proceedings.
04 | LOAN INTEREST, INTEREST ON OVERDUE PRINCIPAL AND LATE-PAYMENT INTEREST
4.1. Interest under civil loan agreements
For loans of property other than credit agreements, Article 468 of the 2015 Civil Code generally caps the agreed interest rate at 20% per annum, unless another law provides otherwise. A loan for which no interest is agreed must be distinguished from one under which interest is payable, but the rate is unspecified. Upon default, Article 466 and Resolution No. 01/2019/NQ-HĐTP distinguish interest accruing during the loan term, interest for late payment of interest already due, and interest on overdue principal. For an interest-bearing loan, interest on overdue principal is calculated under the mechanism of 150% of the contractual lending rate, unless otherwise lawfully agreed. [2], [4]
4.2. Interest arising from delayed performance of a monetary obligation
Article 357 of the 2015 Civil Code governs liability for delayed payment of monetary obligations falling within its scope. It is necessary to identify the amount actually due, the period of delay and the interest rate under a lawful agreement or, in the absence of an agreement, clause 2 Article 468. The formula for overdue interest under a loan agreement must not be applied indiscriminately to all payment obligations. [2]
4.3. Late-payment interest in commercial transactions and case law
In circumstances governed by Article 306 of the 2005 Commercial Law, the aggrieved party may claim interest on overdue payments for goods, service remuneration and other reasonable expenses, unless otherwise agreed or provided by law. Under Article 11 of Resolution No. 01/2019/NQ-HĐTP, where a court determines the rate under Article 306, the relevant benchmark is the average overdue-debt interest rate of at least three commercial banks operating in the locality where the court hears the case, at the time of the first-instance hearing. There is no automatic default rate of 10% per annum. [4], [5]
Case Law No. 09/2016/AL, developed from Cassation Decision No. 07/2013/KDTM-GĐT dated 15 March 2013, illustrates the requirement to use the average overdue-debt interest rate rather than the base interest rate announced by the State Bank of Vietnam. It also illustrates that, in the circumstances of that case, interest under Article 306 was not to be charged on contractual penalties or damages. The case law should not be extended into a general rule governing every payment under every type of contract. [6]
4.4. Reviewing the interest claim
An incorrect outstanding balance, due date or interest regime may result in a reduction of the amount claimed or the rejection of part of the claim. Interest accrued before adjudication must be distinguished from the obligation to continue paying interest after judgment under the applicable rules. The parties’ agreement, partial payments and day-count convention must also be examined.
05 | CONTRACTUAL PENALTIES: CONDITIONS OF APPLICATION AND CALCULATION BASE
Contractual penalties generally require an agreement between the parties. For contracts governed by the Commercial Law, Article 301 limits the penalty to 8% of the value of the breached portion of the contractual obligation, subject to statutory exceptions. A provision stipulating a penalty of 20% of the entire contract price does not automatically establish an entitlement to that amount. The obligation breached, the corresponding value and the conditions for imposing the penalty must be identified precisely. [5]
Construction contracts require particular attention to legislative changes: clause 3 Article 86 of the 2025 Law on Construction (effective from 1 July 2026) provides for a ceiling of 12% of the value of the breached portion of the contract in respect of construction works under public investment projects and public-private partnership (PPP) projects. Cluase 2 Article 146 of the 2014 Law on Construction should not be cited as a generally applicable provision currently in force, nor should the 12% ceiling be applied to all construction contracts. For contracts entered into before the new law took effect, the transitional provisions and the time at which rights and obligations arose must be assessed. [7]
The penalty rate stated in the contract is only the starting point. The enforceability of the penalty claim depends on proving the breach, identifying the party subject to the penalty and complying with the statutory limits.
06 | DAMAGES: MORE THAN AN ESTIMATE OF LOSS
Establishing that a counterparty has breached a contract does not mean that the entire amount claimed as damages will be awarded. Under Articles 360, 361 and 419 of the 2015 Civil Code, liability for damages must be assessed by reference to loss caused by the breach of an obligation, including actual loss and the benefits the entitled party would otherwise have obtained under the contract, to the extent permitted by law. [2]
For commercial transactions, Articles 302–304 of the 2005 Commercial Law require proof of the breach, actual loss and a direct causal relationship. Damages may include actual and direct loss and the direct benefits that would otherwise have been obtained; however, projected revenue must not automatically be equated with lost profit. Exemptions from liability and the obligation to take reasonable measures to mitigate loss under Articles 294 and 305 must also be considered. [5]
For example, where a buyer wrongfully refuses to accept goods, the seller may claim storage costs, additional transport costs and lost profits if it can establish their basis, amount and causal connection with the breach, while avoiding double counting of loss.
For contracts in regulated sectors, the scope of damages must also be assessed against sector-specific provisions. In particular, Article 86 of the 2025 Law on Construction addresses agreements on damages in construction contracts and the basis for determining loss, including actual loss, predetermined damages corresponding to the breached obligation and the extent of the breach. Accordingly, no single damages formula can be applied mechanically to every type of contract.
A substantiated claim for damages depends not on the amount declared by the business, but on its ability to prove each item of loss, the basis on which liability arises and the scope of compensation permitted by law.
CONCLUSION |
A legal strategy for protecting receivables does not begin by adding together principal, interest, contractual penalties and damages. It begins by determining who is liable; which obligations have fallen due; which amounts are supported by sufficient evidence; which remedies are available; and which assets may lawfully be realised. Early legal assessment enables businesses to quantify risk, formulate consistent claims and prepare more effectively for dispute resolution.
