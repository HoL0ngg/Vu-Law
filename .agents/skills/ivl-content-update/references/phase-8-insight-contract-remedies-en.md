CONTRACT NEGOTIATIONS: DO CONTRACTUAL REMEDIES ADEQUATELY PROTECT BUSINESSES?
Contractual penalties, damages, late-payment interest and termination rights—legal distinctions to address at the negotiating table.
In Part 1, we examined three foundational issues in contract negotiations: the requirements for a transaction to be legally valid, authority to represent a party, and the definition of contractual obligations. Once these matters are clarified, another question becomes particularly important: what remedies may a business pursue if its counterparty breaches the contract?
A more stringent clause does not necessarily afford greater protection. The effectiveness of a contractual remedy depends on the applicable law, the conditions for invoking it, the extent of the obligation breached and the evidence the business can produce.
04 | A PENALTY OF 20% OF THE CONTRACT VALUE: IS THE STIPULATED AMOUNT RECOVERABLE?
Article 418 of the 2015 Civil Code provides that contractual penalties are determined by agreement, unless otherwise prescribed by relevant legislation. In relationships governed by the 2005 Commercial Law, Articles 300 and 301 prescribe the conditions for imposing contractual penalties and, generally, a ceiling of 8% of the value of the contractual obligation breached. The exception referred to in Article 266 and any applicable sector-specific provisions must also be considered.
Accordingly, a clause providing for a “penalty of 20% of the total contract value” does not, of itself, guarantee recovery of that amount. The governing law, the conduct constituting the breach, the value of the obligation breached and any applicable exceptions must be properly identified. Equally, it should not automatically be assumed that the entire clause is invalid merely because the stated percentage exceeds the limit under a particular legal regime.
A contractual remedies clause should reflect the legal framework governing the transaction, rather than merely stipulating a substantial penalty as a negotiating lever.
Key consideration: Examine the basis for imposing a penalty, the penalty rate, the value of the obligation breached and the interaction between contractual penalties and other remedies.
05 | WHERE A CONTRACTUAL PENALTY IS AGREED, MAY DAMAGES ALSO BE CLAIMED?
Contractual penalties and damages are distinct legal remedies. Whether they may be pursued concurrently depends on the nature of the legal relationship and the provisions governing the contract.
Under Article 418(3) of the 2015 Civil Code, where the parties agree only on a contractual penalty, without also agreeing that damages may be claimed, the default position under that provision is that the breaching party is liable only for the penalty. Any different rule under relevant legislation must nevertheless be considered. By contrast, Article 307(2) of the 2005 Commercial Law provides that, where a contractual penalty has been agreed, the aggrieved party may seek both the penalty and damages, unless that Law provides otherwise.
The right to claim damages does not, however, mean that every alleged loss is recoverable. In commercial matters, Articles 302–305 of the 2005 Commercial Law address the scope of compensable loss, the grounds for liability, the burden of proving loss and the duty to mitigate loss.
Key consideration: Define the contractual remedies clearly and retain evidence of the breach, the loss sustained, causation and reasonable steps taken to mitigate that loss.
06 | THE COUNTERPARTY PAYS LATE: HOW IS LATE-PAYMENT INTEREST CALCULATED?
Late payment may give rise to an obligation to pay interest, but no single interest rate applies to every contract. Article 357 of the 2015 Civil Code addresses liability for delayed performance of an obligation to pay money; the applicable interest rate must be considered in light of Article 468 and any lawful agreement between the parties.
Where Article 306 of the 2005 Commercial Law applies, the aggrieved party may be entitled to interest on the overdue sum, calculated by reference to the average overdue debt interest rate prevailing in the market at the time of payment and the period of delay, unless otherwise agreed or provided by law. Article 11 of Resolution No. 01/2019/NQ-HĐTP provides guidance on determining that rate by reference to at least three commercial banks in the locality where the court hears the case.
The absence of an express late-payment interest provision therefore does not necessarily deprive a party of the right to claim interest. Conversely, stipulating a rate without regard to the applicable legal regime may itself give rise to a dispute over the amount recoverable.
Key consideration: Specify when payment falls due, the conditions triggering payment, the documents evidencing outstanding debt and an interest-calculation mechanism appropriate to the transaction.
07 | THE COUNTERPARTY BREACHES: MAY THE CONTRACT BE UNILATERALLY TERMINATED OR CANCELLED?
A breach does not automatically entitle a party to terminate performance unilaterally or cancel the contract. These are distinct legal mechanisms, with different grounds and consequences. Articles 423, 427 and 428 of the 2015 Civil Code must be considered in their respective contexts, including the grounds and consequences of cancellation, the grounds for unilateral termination, notice requirements and resulting liability.
In commercial transactions, Articles 310 and 312 of the 2005 Commercial Law govern suspension of contractual performance and cancellation where the breach is one the parties have agreed will trigger the relevant remedy, or constitutes a fundamental breach of a contractual obligation, subject to the exemptions from liability under Article 294. The notice requirements and legal consequences under Articles 311 and 313–315 must also be applied as appropriate.
As a general principle, exercising a legally established right to terminate or cancel a contract does not require a prior court ruling. If a dispute arises, however, the grounds for doing so, the procedure followed and the manner in which the right was exercised may be scrutinised. Sending a notice does not, in itself, establish that termination or cancellation was lawful; a party that acts without sufficient grounds may itself incur liability.
Key consideration: Distinguish temporary suspension of performance, suspension of contractual performance, unilateral termination and cancellation; agree on the applicable triggers, notice requirements, effective dates and treatment of outstanding obligations.

They are also a critical stage for defining rights and obligations, allocating responsibility and anticipating the legal consequences of breach.
