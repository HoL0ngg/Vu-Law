OVERDUE RECEIVABLES: NEGOTIATE, LITIGATE, OR ADOPT AN ALTERNATIVE STRATEGY?
TOPIC 01: DISPUTE RESOLUTION STRATEGY
An effective legal strategy should not merely establish an entitlement to payment; it must also account for whether that entitlement can be realised in practice.
In a commercial transaction, goods may have been delivered, services rendered and invoices issued, yet payment remains outstanding. When a receivable falls overdue, businesses often face a choice between initiating proceedings and continuing negotiations. Framing the issue as a binary choice, however, may obscure more fundamental considerations: the commercial objective, the available evidence and the obligor’s capacity to pay.
According to the Report on Civil Judgment Enforcement Results for the 12-month period of 2025 (1 October 2024–30 September 2025), 1,064,419 enforcement matters were recorded nationwide, of which 685,898 (64.44%) were classified as having conditions for enforcement. These figures concern civil judgment enforcement generally, rather than recovery rates in commercial disputes. Nevertheless, they illustrate the importance of distinguishing a legally sustainable claim, a favourable judgment and the practical ability to secure performance of the obligation. [1]
1 | ASSESSING OVERDUE RECEIVABLES BEFORE DETERMINING A COURSE OF ACTION
The starting point is not necessarily to issue a payment demand or prepare a statement of claim, but to identify precisely the relief sought, the party against whom it is sought and the underlying legal basis. Contracts, addenda, purchase orders, delivery and acceptance records, invoices, banking records, correspondence and account reconciliations should be reviewed collectively. An invoice or an isolated statement of account does not necessarily establish every aspect of the existence or extent of a payment obligation.
Particular attention should be given to the legal capacity and authority of the person acknowledging the obligation. A document signed by a duly authorised representative may carry significant evidential weight; however, a document signed by an employee or an individual without a separate written power of attorney is not necessarily devoid of evidential value. Its probative weight may also depend on that person’s actual role, the parties’ course of dealing, the company’s approval or ratification and other evidence assessed under the applicable rules on representation and civil procedure. [2]
In addition to determining the outstanding principal, businesses should distinguish interest, contractual penalties, damages and any properly substantiated deductions or set-offs. Allegations of defective goods, delayed delivery or unaccepted services may give rise to counterclaims or defences to a payment claim. A sound strategy therefore requires scrutiny of the counterparty’s position, not merely the claimant’s own records.
A comprehensive assessment should also consider the obligor’s assets and financial capacity, within the limits of lawfully accessible information, together with the rights of secured creditors and other third parties. The likelihood of obtaining a favourable decision and the prospects of actual recovery must be assessed separately.
2 | NEGOTIATION: WHEN TO CONTINUE AND WHEN TO WALK AWAY
Negotiation may reduce costs, preserve commercial relationships and facilitate earlier payment. Its value, however, lies not in the number of meetings held or assurances received, but in whether commitments can be translated into clear, time-bound and verifiable obligations.
Where an obligor faces liquidity constraints but remains cooperative, a business may consider instalment arrangements, a defined repayment schedule, default provisions, written confirmation of the outstanding balance and suitable security. Where a pledge, mortgage or guarantee is contemplated, the parties should examine validity requirements, authority, title to the relevant assets and any registration or other formalities required for effectiveness against third parties. A written undertaking to pay is not, by itself, an assurance of recovery.
Conversely, persistent delays, failure to disclose relevant information, repudiation of previously acknowledged liabilities or indications of asset transfers may materially diminish the value of further negotiations. Such indications must be verified: non-payment alone should not be equated with fraud or asset dissipation.
3 | CAN PROTRACTED NEGOTIATIONS AFFECT THE LIMITATION PERIOD?
The applicable limitation period depends on the nature of the dispute and the governing law. Article 319 of the 2005 Commercial Law prescribes a two-year limitation period for commercial disputes, running from the date on which lawful rights and interests are infringed, subject to the exception under point (e), clause 1, Article 237. For contractual disputes governed by the 2015 Civil Code, Article 429 prescribes a three-year period from the date on which the claimant knew or ought to have known of the infringement of its lawful rights and interests. A uniform limitation period should not be assumed for all receivables; sector-specific legislation and exceptions to the application of limitation periods must also be examined.
Under clause 2, Article 149 of the 2015 Civil Code and Article 184 of the 2015 Civil Procedure Code, a court applies a limitation period only upon a party’s request made before the first-instance court delivers its judgment or decision disposing of the matter. Expiry of the period for bringing a claim does not automatically extinguish the underlying payment obligation. The procedural effect of a limitation defence depends on the circumstances and the nature of the claim.
Article 157 of the 2015 Civil Code provides that the limitation period for instituting a civil action recommences where: (i) the obligor acknowledges part or all of its obligation to the claimant; (ii) the obligor acknowledges or has fully performed part of its obligation to the claimant; or (iii) the parties have reached a settlement through voluntary conciliation. The new period begins on the day following the relevant event. Not every exchange of correspondence, request for an extension or partial payment necessarily satisfies these statutory conditions; the substance and supporting evidence of each act must be examined.
Businesses should maintain a clear record of payment due dates, the emergence of the dispute, acknowledgements of liability and the anticipated expiry of applicable limitation periods. Negotiations should never proceed without monitoring these critical deadlines.
4 | LITIGATION OR ARBITRATION: SPEED IS NOT THE ONLY CONSIDERATION
Parties do not have an unrestricted choice to refer every dispute to arbitration. It is necessary to ascertain whether the dispute is arbitrable under the 2010 Law on Commercial Arbitration, whether a valid arbitration agreement exists and whether that agreement is incapable of performance. Article 6 requires courts to decline to accept a dispute where an arbitration agreement exists, subject to the statutory exceptions. [4]
Arbitration may be suitable where confidentiality and the finality of an award are important. Its duration and cost, however, depend on the applicable rules, the composition of the tribunal, the complexity of the dispute, evidentiary issues and jurisdictional objections. Although an arbitral award is final, it remains susceptible to an application to set aside on statutory grounds. Arbitration should therefore not be presumed invariably faster or more costly than court proceedings.
Article 49 of the 2010 Law on Commercial Arbitration authorises arbitral tribunals to grant certain interim measures, while Article 53 governs applications to the courts for such measures. Court intervention is therefore not invariably required. The nature of the relief sought, the timing of the application, the competent decision-maker and statutory limitations must be assessed.
In considering litigation, businesses should assess the court’s jurisdiction, its ability to determine related claims, evidentiary procedures, adjudication and enforcement. Forum selection should be consistent with the contractual dispute resolution clause and the ultimate objective of securing an enforceable outcome.
5 | MULTI-TIER DISPUTE RESOLUTION CLAUSES: USEFUL, BUT DRAFTING IS CRITICAL
Many contracts require the parties to negotiate for a specified period, then attempt mediation, before referring the dispute to arbitration or litigation. Such arrangements may facilitate an early resolution, but can also generate disputes as to whether a mandatory pre-arbitration or pre-litigation step has been satisfied.
The legal effect of a multi-tier clause must be assessed by reference to its precise terms and the designated forum. It is important to distinguish: (i) a contractual obligation to negotiate or mediate; (ii) a condition precedent to commencing litigation or arbitration; and (iii) a statutory ground for setting aside an arbitral award. Article 68 of the 2010 Law on Commercial Arbitration includes, among the grounds for setting aside an award, non-compliance of arbitral procedure with the parties’ agreement or the Law. Nevertheless, omission of a negotiation step does not automatically constitute such a ground.
Vietnamese case law reflects an evolution in judicial approaches to pre-arbitration negotiation and mediation requirements. In Decision No. 10/2014/QĐ-PQTT dated 28 October 2014, the People’s Court of Hanoi set aside an arbitral award after a party failed to undertake the contractually agreed negotiation step, treating the omission as a serious procedural violation under point (b), clause 2, Article 68 of the 2010 Law on Commercial Arbitration. In contrast, in Decisions No. 02/2020/QĐ-PQTT dated 23 April 2020 and No. 04/2022/QĐ-PQTT dated 21 March 2022, the Court declined to set aside arbitral awards merely because negotiation or mediation had not taken place before arbitration.
These decisions suggest an emerging distinction between contractual pre-arbitration obligations and arbitral procedure: failure to negotiate or mediate does not necessarily deprive a party of access to arbitration or, without more, justify setting aside an award. [5]
Accordingly, to minimise the risk of protracted challenges, multi-tier clauses should specify when a dispute is deemed to arise, notice requirements, negotiation periods, the point at which each stage is completed, confidentiality obligations and the ultimate dispute resolution forum. The parties should also consider expressly preserving the right to seek interim measures or take other lawful steps to protect their interests.
Where the parties agree to commercial mediation, Decree No. 22/2017/NĐ-CP is a relevant legal instrument. Out-of-court commercial mediation should not be conflated with court-annexed conciliation or arbitral procedure. A multi-tier clause should identify the duration and completion of each stage and its effect on the right to commence proceedings, while avoiding conflicting arbitration and court jurisdiction provisions.
6 | SHOULD NEGOTIATIONS CONTINUE AFTER PROCEEDINGS HAVE BEEN COMMENCED?
Commencing proceedings does not necessarily preclude settlement. In court proceedings, the parties may continue negotiations and participate in conciliation in accordance with the applicable procedural rules, except where conciliation is prohibited or cannot be conducted. If a lawful settlement is reached and procedural requirements are satisfied, the court may issue a decision recognising the parties’ agreement. Such a decision is distinct from meeting minutes or a payment undertaking executed outside the proceedings. [2]
The key considerations extend beyond the agreed amount to include payment deadlines, the calculation of any outstanding balance, consequences of default, obligations relating to secured assets and the availability of enforcement in the event of non-compliance. A settlement that appears commercially attractive but lacks an effective implementation mechanism may simply prolong the dispute.

CONCLUSION | A STRATEGY MUST DELIVER PRACTICAL RESULTS
Negotiation need not signify an unprincipled concession, just as litigation does not, of itself, guarantee recovery. A sound strategy calls for a combined assessment of the legal claim, the strength of the evidence, limitation periods, the appropriate forum and the practical conditions for performance. Crucially, it should remain responsive to new information rather than be fixed at the outset.
