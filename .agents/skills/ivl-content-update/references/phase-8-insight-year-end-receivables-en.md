AT YEAR-END, WHICH OUTSTANDING RECEIVABLES SHOULD BUSINESSES PRIORITISE? CRITERIA FOR PROTECTING CASH FLOW AND RECOVERY RIGHTS
At year-end, businesses often need to balance employee salaries, tax obligations, payments to suppliers and capital planning for the following year simultaneously. When multiple customers are late in making payments, prioritising only the largest outstanding debt or sending payment reminders en masse may not necessarily be effective.
The more important management question is: which receivables are at risk of losing the right to bring a claim, becoming uncollectible or having the greatest impact on cash flow if action is further delayed? Businesses should classify their receivables portfolios according to legal urgency, the practical likelihood of recovery, the quality of evidence, the costs of recovery and the value of commercial relationships. These are proposed management criteria, not a legally mandatory order of priority.
01 | WHICH RECEIVABLES ARE AT RISK OF EXPIRY OF THE LIMITATION PERIOD FOR INITIATING LEGAL PROCEEDINGS?
Article 319 of the 2005 Commercial Law provides that, in principle, the limitation period for initiating legal proceedings in commercial disputes is two years from the date on which lawful rights or interests were infringed, unless otherwise provided by law. For civil contractual disputes governed by Article 429 of the 2015 Civil Code, the limitation period is three years from the date on which the person entitled to bring a claim knew or ought to have known that their lawful rights or interests were infringed. Businesses should correctly determine the nature of the dispute and the starting point of the limitation period before choosing a course of action. [1][2]
Receivables at risk of becoming time-barred should be reviewed immediately. Under Article 157 of the 2015 Civil Code, acknowledgement of an obligation by the obligor, performance of part of the obligation or voluntary reconciliation between the parties may cause the limitation period to recommence where the statutory conditions are met; an extension of the payment deadline does not automatically have this effect. Under Clause 2, Article 149 of the 2015 Civil Code, a Court applies the limitation period only upon a valid request by a party made before the first-instance Court issues its judgment or decision resolving the matter. Businesses should not wait until year-end to review limitation periods. [2]
02 | DOES THE COUNTERPARTY SHOW SIGNS OF DECLINING PAYMENT CAPACITY OR TRANSFERRING ASSETS?
Indicators worth examining include late payments to multiple creditors, cessation of operations at the registered address, emerging disputes or unusual changes involving material assets. However, a change of legal representative, transfer of capital or cash-flow difficulties do not, in themselves, establish asset dissipation. Businesses should verify information from lawful sources and assess each situation on its own facts.
Where there are grounds to believe that enforcement of a judgment may be adversely affected, businesses should preserve evidence, assess the prospects of negotiation and prepare a timely litigation strategy. Applications for interim urgent measures, such as freezing accounts or prohibiting the transfer of rights over property, must satisfy the applicable requirements under the 2015 Civil Procedure Code and Resolution No. 02/2020/NQ-HĐTP. Not every application will have sufficient grounds to be granted by the Court; the relevant statutory requirements must be met. [3]
03 | ASSESSING EVIDENCE: IS THERE SUFFICIENT EVIDENCE TO INITIATE LEGAL PROCEEDINGS?
Before selecting a course of action, businesses should review the chain of documents evidencing the transaction, performance of obligations, amounts already paid and the actual outstanding balance:
Relatively complete documentation: Contracts, purchase orders, delivery or acceptance records, bank statements, invoices and consistent debt reconciliations are important documents. However, no single document automatically guarantees success in litigation; the authenticity of the evidence, the signatory’s authority and the consistency of the documents must still be assessed.
Incomplete or inconsistent documentation: Priority should be given to reconciling outstanding balances, obtaining written confirmation from an authorised person and preserving original data from emails, messages and electronic transactions. A partial payment by the counterparty may constitute evidence of an obligation, but a nominal payment should not be solicited solely to manufacture misleading evidence. Measures to protect the right to initiate legal proceedings should not be delayed merely to complete the documentation.
04 | IS THE PRACTICAL LIKELIHOOD OF RECOVERY PROPORTIONATE TO THE COSTS INVOLVED?
The ultimate objective of a business in a receivables dispute is not merely to obtain a favourable judgment on paper, but to recover funds into its account. Even a successful outcome before a Court or arbitral tribunal may have no economic value to the business if the obligor has no assets available for enforcement; the costs of resolving the dispute must also be considered. A favourable judgment or arbitral award does not necessarily mean that the business will recover its money.
Therefore, before taking legal action such as initiating proceedings, businesses should consider the ability to identify assets lawfully belonging to the obligor, assets already provided as security to other creditors, the expected timeframe, court fees and litigation costs, enforcement costs and the impact on business operations. The book value of a receivable should be distinguished from the amount realistically recoverable.
For counterparties that remain operational, demonstrate willingness to pay and can substantiate their repayment cash flow, businesses may consider a repayment schedule, partial advance payment and legally valid security. The agreement should clearly specify the outstanding balance, payment deadlines, interest and the consequences of default on each instalment.
For counterparties avoiding their obligations or presenting a risk of asset transfers, businesses should assess litigation and appropriate asset-protection measures rather than continuing to grant unconditional extensions. For a relatively small receivable supported by clear documentation, where the counterparty has the ability to pay, a prompt negotiated resolution may sometimes be more effective than prolonged proceedings.

Group 1: Legally urgent
Approaching expiry of the limitation period; specific evidence of a risk of asset transfers or a decline in the ability to satisfy an enforceable judgment.
Check the limitation period; preserve evidence; consider initiating proceedings and applying for interim urgent measures where the statutory conditions are met.
Group 2: Potential for prompt recovery
The debt is clearly established; the counterparty still has cash flow or the ability to pay; recovery costs are reasonable.
Set a specific payment deadline; negotiate immediate payment or controlled instalment payments; prepare legal action in the event of default.
Group 3: Evidence needs strengthening
Delivery records are missing, balances have not been reconciled or the obligation remains disputed.
Reconcile outstanding balances, verify the authority of relevant persons and preserve electronic data; monitor the limitation period at the same time.
Group 4: Conditional restructuring
An important counterparty is experiencing temporary difficulties but has a repayment plan and verifiable sources of funds.
Agree in writing on the repayment schedule, interest, security and an acceleration clause making the entire outstanding balance due upon default.
Group 5: High risk of loss
Payment capacity is very low; the counterparty has ceased operations or there is information concerning dissolution, recovery or bankruptcy proceedings.
Assess costs and benefits; consider petitioning the Court to commence bankruptcy proceedings against the counterparty in accordance with the procedures prescribed by the 2025 Law on Recovery and Bankruptcy. [4] Monitor recovery and bankruptcy proceedings and the deadlines for declaring claims; consider accounting and tax treatment subject to the specific legal requirements. Do not automatically treat the debt as irrecoverable.
06 | CONCLUSION
There is no single order for handling receivables that suits every business. The receivable to prioritise is not necessarily the largest, but rather one for which the risk increases rapidly if action is delayed or which can be converted into cash at a reasonable cost. Businesses should maintain a receivables register for each counterparty, recording the outstanding balance, age of the debt, estimated limitation period, evidential position, payment capacity, security and next action; this register should be updated regularly rather than reviewed only at year-end closing.
